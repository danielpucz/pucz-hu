Perhaps being a professional used to mean knowing things. Lots of things. Preferably things other people didn't know, explained confidently while standing next to a very large screen. Add some numbers, a diagram with arrows, and a reasonably expensive shirt, and you were practically unstoppable. I spent years trying to become one of those people. With varying degrees of success, mostly depending on the shirt.

A proper little [Man of Seriousity](https://pucz.hu/blog/man-of-seriousity/). Or at least an aspiring one, learning the language of people who owned expensive notebooks and knew what to do with them. And I rather enjoyed it. I liked learning things, understanding things, knowing why something worked and why something else didn't. I particularly liked the occasional moment when somebody would say "Oh, that actually makes sense" and I could pretend that the preceding forty-five minutes of confused gesticulating had all been part of the plan. Which, in my defence, sometimes they were. Other times I was just drawing circles until the answer appeared. A surprisingly effective technique, considering how little circles know about business.

And for a very long time, this seemed to be how the world worked. We built entire professions around knowing things and being able to explain them. Companies spent extraordinary amounts of money teaching people what their problems were, how to recognise them, and why their particular brand of problem-solving was the most appropriate one. We called it educating the market, thought leadership, building authority, and sometimes, when we were feeling particularly creative, a white paper. We made presentations about presentations. We held conferences where people explained things to other people who would then go home and explain those things to slightly different people. It was a magnificent international recycling programme for expertise, and I say this with affection because I've participated in it quite enthusiastically. I've even made diagrams with circles. Several of them. Some had arrows. One had an arrow pointing to another arrow, which I believe qualifies as senior management.

And a lot of it was genuinely valuable. People really did know things. They had spent years learning their craft, getting things wrong, correcting them, getting them wrong in new and exciting ways, and eventually becoming rather good at what they did. I was lucky enough to work with some of them. The sort of people who could look at a problem for five minutes and identify something you hadn't noticed in five weeks. The ones who didn't need to explain how clever they were because you could see it in the work. I loved these people. I still do. There's something terribly attractive about somebody being quietly excellent at something. Even if that something happens to be spreadsheets, which is perhaps the least romantic collection of rectangles ever invented.

But somewhere along the way, we also became rather good at imitating the appearance of knowing. Which was understandable. If the world rewards people for looking clever, people will naturally become interested in looking clever. Some of us learned the appropriate vocabulary. Others developed very impressive facial expressions for meetings. I personally perfected a thoughtful-looking nod that could mean anything from "I completely agree" to "I wonder if penguins have knees." Entire brands discovered that sounding intelligent was considerably cheaper than actually inventing something useful. We made a little theatre out of professionalism, complete with costumes, scripts, and a surprisingly complicated ticketing system.

And now, rather suddenly, the theatre appears to have acquired an unlimited number of actors.

You can ask a blinking rectangle to explain an industry, write a business strategy, produce a reasonably convincing opinion, or turn your half-formed shower thought into a presentation that looks as though a consulting firm has spent three months and the GDP of a small island nation preparing it. Everybody can suddenly sound extremely professional. Everybody can have a framework. Everybody can have a point of view, a beautifully written introduction, and a conclusion that somehow involves the word transformation. It's wonderful and strange. It’s terrifying. We have essentially given the entire population a very expensive-looking suit, and now we're standing around wondering why everybody looks like a management consultant. Including the management consultants, who must be having a particularly confusing time.

I love that people can suddenly do things they couldn't do before. That ideas which might have remained stuck inside somebody's head can become something visible, shareable. I love that the distance between imagining something and making a first version of it is getting ridiculously short. I've had ideas that lived in notebooks for years because I didn't have the time, money of patience required to turn them into anything. Now some of those ideas feel almost embarrassingly possible. Like finding out that the mountain you've been preparing to climb has acquired an escalator. A slightly unreliable escalator that occasionally invents another mountains, but an escalator nonetheless. And after spending a significant portion of my life trying to get ideas out of my head and into the real world, I'm quite happy about the escalator.

I keep wondering what happens when looking like you know something becomes practically free.

Because for quite a while, the performance itself was valuable. If you could explain things convincingly, people would listen. If you could produce the right materials, use the right vocabulary, and maintain the appropriate level of professional seriousness, people might assume that something worthwhile was happening underneath. And often there was. But not always. Sometimes there was just a very handsome presentation having an absolutely wonderful time being a presentation. Twenty-seven slides of premium-grade importantness. With a roadmap. And a vision. And a picture of a mountain, an airplane, or middle aged George Clooney for reasons that were never entirely explained.

I wonder if we're approaching a rather funny little reversal. What if the things we've spent so much time learning to imitate become the least interesting things about us? What if a beautiful explanation stops being especially impressive, simply because beautiful explanations are everywhere? What if the question slowly changes from "Does this person sound like they know what they're doing?" to something much more inconvenient, like "[Do they actually give a fuck](https://pucz.hu/blog/people-giving-a-fuck/)?"

I realise this sounds dangerously close to one of those motivational speeches where a man in a suspiciously tight shirt tells you that passion is the only currency that matters. It isn't. I would still very much like to be paid in actual currency, preferably before the invoice becomes old enough to attend school. But there's something enormously appealing about a world where being useful might gradually become more interesting than appearing useful. Where asking a genuinely good question is worth more than having a spectacular answer to the wrong one. Where somebody who cares about the little things might have an unexpectedly good time.

Is it finally the time for people who cannot quite leave things alone? The ones who will spend two hours investigating something that was supposed to take ten minutes, not because anyone asked them to, but because they noticed something interesting and now apparently we all live here. People who can explain the entire history of a particular design decision but have absolutely no idea where they put their lunch. People who notice the slightly uncomfortable silence after a meeting, or the tiny inconsistency in a product that everybody else has learned to ignore. There's a particular look these people give each other when they meet. A tiny moment of mutual recognition, usually followed by an unnecessarily detailed conversation about something neither of them was supposed to be doing. I love that look. It's like two undercover detectives discovering they're both investigating the same missing teaspoon.

Some days this is a wonderful way to work. Other days it's an extremely elaborate method of not answering your emails. I know this because I have conducted extensive personal research, mostly without meaning to.

I used to think that this kind of attention was a fairly questionable business model. I was already trying to understand what it meant to turn the things I loved doing into something that could support other people, pay bills, and occasionally survive a Tuesday. The funny thing about creative work is that the things which make you good at it aren't necessarily the things that make you good at running a business around it. You can care deeply about the smallest detail in the world and still forget to send the invoice for the entire world. Excellent attention to detail, provided the detail is completely unrelated to the task at hand.

But perhaps there is something interesting about having an unreasonable relationship with the things you care about. Perhaps, in a world becoming increasingly good at producing convincing approximations of almost everything, the people who simply cannot stop asking why might become rather useful. Not because they're better people. Just because there's a difference between knowing how something is supposed to look and having spent a ridiculous amount of your life trying to understand how it actually works. And perhaps that ridiculousness is not quite as ridiculous as it used to be. 

And perhaps the most wonderful part is that none of this requires anybody else to lose.

I've sometimes worried that the world was slowly becoming a competition in who could be the most convincing person in the room. The most confident, the most certain, the most spectacularly self-assured. That eventually we'd all be working for some enormous, charming narcissist who had discovered how to turn everybody else's ideas into a very profitable personal brand. A sort of Big Bad Professional Spider, sitting comfortably in the middle of an enormous corporate web, collecting achievements and occasionally posting about the importance of teamwork. I don't particularly enjoy this picture of the future. It seems unnecessarily exhausting, and I suspect the spider would insist on a weekly alignment meeting.

But what if the future isn't necessarily going in that direction? What if, as the performance becomes easier and easier to reproduce, the performance itself starts losing its magic? What if we eventually become less fascinated by people who appear to have all the answers, and more interested in people who are genuinely trying to find them? What if it’s about presence? Not a great reckoning, not the final defeat of the Big Bad Professional Spider, just a small and rather lovely change in what we find impressive. Maybe the spider could even join us. Spiders are excellent at building things, after all. We might just have to ask him to stop calling the web a scalable ecosystem.

I don't know if that's how things will turn out. It's entirely possible that we'll simply produce more impressive nonsense, faster than ever before, until the entire internet becomes one enormous presentation congratulating itself on its excellent presentation skills. The slides will be beautiful, the messages will be clear, and somewhere on slide 114 we'll discover that nobody remembers what the presentation was supposed to be about. That would be a disappointing outcome, although from a purely typographic perspective I'm sure it would have some interesting moments.

But I keep finding reasons to be cheerful. I keep meeting people who care about things in ways that are difficult to explain, let alone imitate. People who are curious when they could be certain, generous when they could be impressive, and wonderfully enthusiastic about details that nobody else has even noticed. People who don't always know how to present themselves, but who can make something better simply by being there. People who will happily spend their entire afternoon making something slightly more understandable for a person they'll probably never meet. Even when they make meetings longer. Especially when they make meetings longer for a good reason.

And I wonder if we might be getting closer to a rather pleasant sort of professional un-professionalisation. A time when we don't need to spend quite so much energy looking knowledgeable, because knowledge-shaped things are available in unlimited quantities. When the most interesting part of someone's work isn't how polished it looks, but the slightly peculiar human being behind it. Their questions, their strange little obsessions, their occasional terrible ideas, their ability to notice something that wasn't supposed to be noticed. All those beautifully inconvenient things that make working with other people so interesting, and occasionally so very difficult.

Maybe the next version of professionalism isn't about looking like the smartest person in the room. Maybe it's about being comfortable enough not to. Maybe all these wonderful tools will make it easier for more people to participate, while making it a little harder to confuse participating with contributing. Maybe we'll finally become better at recognising the difference between something that looks finished and something somebody genuinely cared enough to finish. I quite like that possibility. Mostly because I've always been rather bad at looking finished myself.

Or maybe I'm just romanticising the whole thing because I happen to like curious people, unfinished ideas, and the sort of conversations that accidentally last three hours. That's also entirely possible.

Either way, there's something rather funny about spending half your life learning how to look like you know what you're doing, only to arrive at a point where practically everybody can do that in fifteen seconds. All those years of seriousity, professionality, expertise-building and expensive-shirt-wearing, and suddenly the whole thing is available to anyone with an internet connection and a reasonably specific prompt.

I wonder if we might finally get to concentrate on doing the actual thing.

And if I'm wrong, I can always make a very convincing presentation explaining why I was right all along.
